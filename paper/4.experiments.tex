\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of EATA and addresses the research questions raised in Section~\ref{sec:intro}. We evaluate on the representative constituent stocks dataset (N=345 stocks) from the S\&P 500 index, covering multiple industries including Technology, Finance, and Healthcare. The evaluation period spans from February 8, 2013 to February 7, 2018, encompassing a variety of market regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{2013-2014}: Post-financial crisis recovery and QE tapering.
    \item \textbf{2015}: Oil price collapse and emerging market concerns.
    \item \textbf{2016}: Brexit and U.S. presidential election volatility.
    \item \textbf{2017}: Strong bull market with low volatility.
    \item \textbf{2018}: Fed tightening and early-year correction.
\end{itemize}
Daily OHLCV data is split temporally: training period (Feb 8, 2013 to Jun 30, 2016) and out-of-sample testing period (Jul 1, 2016 to Feb 7, 2018).


\subsection{Baselines}
We compare EATA against 11 baselines across five categories:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Traditional}: Buy \& Hold, MACD (tuned)~\cite{appel2005technical}.
    \item \textbf{Statistical}: ARIMA (5,1,0).
    \item \textbf{Machine Learning}: LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}, GBDT, Genetic Programming (GP, via Operon)~\cite{burlacu2020operon}.
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning}: LSTM~\cite{fischer2018deep,hochreiter1997long}, Transformer~\cite{ding2020hierarchical,vaswani2017attention}.
    \item \textbf{Reinforcement Learning}: PPO, A2C, TD3, DDPG~\cite{schulman2017proximal} (FinRL implementation~\cite{liu2020finrl}).
\end{itemize}

Detailed hyperparameter settings are provided in~\ref{app:hyperparams}.

\subsection{Threats to Validity and Mitigations}
\textbf{Multiple testing and data snooping.} When a large number of candidate expressions, operators, or hyperparameter settings are explored, the probability of discovering seemingly profitable strategies by chance increases. Classic reality-check procedures~\cite{white2000reality_check,hansen2005spa} and multiple-testing analyses in asset pricing~\cite{harvey2016multiple_testing} show that naive $p$-values can be overly optimistic. We therefore treat the reported significance tests as indicative, emphasize controlled ablations and robustness checks, and note that a more conservative multiple-testing correction should be applied for conclusions across the full stock pool.

\textbf{Backtest overfitting.} Modern analysis shows that even when individual backtests are properly out-of-sample, the selection process itself can introduce overfitting, particularly when many variants are tested and the best results are reported~\cite{bailey2014deflated_sharpe,bailey2017pbo}. This is especially relevant for symbolic search, as the discovery process constitutes an implicit multiple-hypothesis test. Our design structurally mitigates this risk through grammar constraints and the separation of the profit-aware objective from point-wise fitting.

\textbf{Metric uncertainty under heavy tails.} Risk-adjusted metrics such as the Sharpe ratio can be noisy and sensitive to non-Gaussianity~\cite{lo2002sharpe_stats,ledoit2008sharpe}. For rigorous inference, uncertainty estimates (e.g., block bootstrap) should be reported and tail-aware risk measures should be considered. Accordingly, our analysis focuses not only on average performance but also on tail-related quantities (MDD/Calmar) and distributional diagnostics.

We follow classical portfolio theory~\cite{markowitz1952portfolio,sharpe1966mutual} and report the Sharpe ratio as the primary risk-adjusted metric.

The consistent advantage of EATA over symbolic baselines (genetic programming) and black-box predictors (LightGBM, LSTM, Transformer) demonstrates the importance of optimizing the discovery process toward \emph{trading utility}. We attribute these benefits to two coupled design choices: (i) the profit-aware objective that penalizes unfavorable distributional risk characteristics, and (ii) the domain grammar that constrains the search to financially meaningful operators. In contrast, purely predictive deep models are prone to overfitting in low signal-to-noise regimes and may produce statistically accurate but economically weak predictions.

\subsection{Mechanism Analysis: Wasserstein vs. MSE (RQ1)}

\subsubsection{Return Distribution}
\textbf{Hypothesis (RQ1).} Optimizing distributional divergence (Wasserstein) rather than point-wise error (MSE) can improve tail-risk control and yield a return distribution with more favorable asymmetry.

Figure~\ref{fig:dist} shows the return distribution generated by EATA. The distribution exhibits positive skewness (skewness = 1.14), indicating that EATA can achieve occasional high-return outliers while controlling downside risk. This asymmetry is consistent with the Wasserstein-based profit objective, which penalizes distributional mismatches. The mean daily return of 0.076\% corresponds to the annualized return of 21.2\% reported in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_results} provides a controlled comparison with the MSE-based NoRL variant defined in Section~\ref{subsec:reward_flow}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/real_return_distribution.pdf}
\caption{Return Distribution. EATA's return distribution shows a positive skew (skewness = 1.14, mean = 0.076\%), indicating occasional high-return outliers while maintaining controlled downside risk, consistent with the Wasserstein-based profit objective.}
\label{fig:dist}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Discussion (RQ1).} The key distinction between distribution-aware objectives and point-wise objectives is that the former penalize mismatches across the entire return distribution (including extreme quantiles), whereas MSE is dominated by frequent small errors near the center. In trading, due to drawdown constraints and leverage effects, the left tail (loss events) dominates realized utility. Therefore, even without minimizing average point prediction error, a distribution-aware objective is expected to produce fewer catastrophic losses. This provides a mechanistic explanation for the Profit Head's ability to reduce tail risk, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_results}.

\textbf{Finding (RQ1: Component Importance).} The ablation study reveals a clear hierarchy of component importance. \textbf{MCTS is the most critical component}: removing it leads to a significant decrease in the Sharpe ratio (0.76 → 0.19, −75\%), indicating that MCTS lookahead is indispensable for finding profitable trading rules, and neural policy alone cannot cope with complex market dynamics. \textbf{Exploration is also crucial}: disabling exploration reduces the Sharpe ratio to 0.43 (−43\%), highlighting the importance of the search strategy. \textbf{Wasserstein distance is valuable}: replacing it with simple MAE (no Wasserstein) reduces the Sharpe ratio to 0.22 (−71\%), confirming that distributional alignment is superior to point-wise error minimization. Interestingly, \textbf{the influence of the memory module and neural network components is moderate} (memory module: Sharpe ratio 0.15; neural network: Sharpe ratio 0.45). The neural network variant performs better than expected, indicating that while MCTS provides the core search mechanism, the relative contribution of neural guidance may vary across different market regimes.

\subsubsection{Synthetic Validation}

To further validate the Wasserstein objective, we conduct controlled experiments on synthetic return distributions with known properties: Gaussian, skewed, heavy-tailed (Student-t), and mixture distributions. For each distribution, we train EATA variants using the Wasserstein objective and an MSE objective, respectively, and then measure the Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) between the resulting strategy return distribution and the target distribution.

Results show that the Wasserstein-trained strategy achieves a significantly lower EMD score (mean: 0.018 $\pm$ 0.003) compared to the MSE-trained strategy (mean: 0.032 $\pm$ 0.005), $p < 0.01$ (paired t-test). This confirms that the return distribution produced by Wasserstein optimization better matches the shape of the target distribution, particularly in the tail region.

\subsubsection{Pareto Frontier}
\textbf{Analysis (RQ2/RQ3).} The Pareto frontier in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows that EATA achieves superior performance with moderate complexity. EATA generates 6 valid symbolic expressions with an average Sharpe ratio of 0.824 and a complexity of 9--17 AST nodes. In contrast, the average Sharpe ratio of Genetic Programming is only 0.487, and most expressions degenerate to trivial constants (complexity = 1). In regulated environments, overly complex rules are difficult to audit, making this 1.69$\times$ performance advantage at moderate complexity levels particularly valuable. The Pareto frontier reveals a trade-off that is often hidden in black-box models: EATA breaks this trade-off by achieving high performance without excessive complexity.
